M.S. Thesis · Carbon-Aware Task Offloading

Deriving transmission power instead of learning it

Minimizing energy and minimizing carbon are not the same objective. In the first hour of the evaluation trace, Seattle's grid runs at 72 gCO₂/kWh and Cheyenne's at 688 — the same kilowatt-hour, ten times the emissions. This thesis puts five edge servers on real hourly grid data and asks them to decide, with no central controller, how much work to compute locally, hand to a neighbor, or push to the cloud.

Python · PyTorch · NumPy Advisor: Li-Hsing Yen NYCU, Institute of Network Engineering 2024 – 2026

In short

Each edge server is an independent agent. Every hour it splits its queue four ways — compute locally, send to a peer, send to the cloud, or leave it queued — trying to minimize long-term carbon emissions without letting its backlog grow without bound. Two solutions are proposed: LAAO, a Lyapunov-based analytical algorithm, and RMAPPO, a recurrent multi-agent PPO policy trained centrally and executed decentrally.

This page covers the one design decision that mattered most and generalizes past the topic: what the neural network is allowed to output. Making the agents choose transmission time rather than transmission power — and deriving the power from the channel equation — turned a physical constraint the policy had to learn into one it cannot violate.

01 · Problem

Two objectives that pull in opposite directions

Carbon-aware scheduling wants to wait for a cleaner hour. Queue stability wants to drain the backlog now. The objective is the long-term time average of one, subject to the other staying bounded.

The thesis formulates this as minimizing long-run average carbon emissions subject to strong stability of every edge and cloud queue. Every hour, each edge server independently decides how to split its own backlog:

The simulated system draws its topology from the Internet Topology Zoo: five edge servers — Seattle, Cheyenne, Chicago, New York, Kansas City — each with its own real hourly carbon trace, offloading horizontally to specific peers and vertically to a cloud server in Berkeley County, South Carolina.

That unevenness and the delay in what agents can see are what make the problem hard:

  • Uneven peersObservation and action dimensions differ per agent, so each gets its own actor and critic sized to its own neighborhood rather than one shared network.
  • Stale observationsAn agent sees its neighbors' queues and the carbon intensities from the previous slot, smoothed by a per-site exponential predictor — but the environment bills its emissions at the true intensity. Agents act on delayed, approximate signals and pay real prices.

02 · The design decision

Let the policy choose time; compute the power from physics

The channel gives transmitted data as Shannon capacity over time. Fix the data, and required power is exponential in the reciprocal of the time you allow it. A neural network can learn that relationship, or it can be handed it.

Moving x bits over a link in t seconds requires a transmission power that rises exponentially as t shrinks, while the energy spent — and thus the carbon — is only linear in both. Compressing a transfer into half the time therefore never pays: the exponential power term dominates the linear saving in time. Stretching every transmission across the entire slot is strictly optimal, and that fact is a closed form, not something that needs to be discovered from reward: 

// marl_related/utils.py — the channel equation, used as a decoder, not a lesson
R_max = W * log2(1 + (g * p_max) / N0)   // most this link can carry
x_actual = min(x_target, R_max * t_alloc)
p_actual = (2**(x_actual / (W * t_alloc)) - 1) * (N0 / g)

Given a data size and a time allocation, the required power falls out. If the requested size exceeds what the link can carry at full power within that time, the size is trimmed to what fits — so the returned pair is always physically realizable.

That leaves two candidate action spaces. The conventional one, XP, has each agent output a data size and a power per link; the environment then works out how long the transfer takes. The proposed one, XT, has each agent output a data size and a time weight per link; the weights are normalized across that agent's active links so they fill exactly one slot, and the power is derived from the equation above. 

// marl_related/action_related/decoders.py — XTDecoder
active_taus = [raw_tau_cloud] + [tau for _, _, tau in raw_x_peers]
total_tau   = sum(active_taus) + 1e-9
t_cloud     = (raw_tau_cloud / total_tau) * SAFE_T_OFF   // shares of one slot

The normalization is the whole trick. Whatever the network emits, the times allocated to one agent's links sum to the slot — the time-budget constraint is not checked, it is unrepresentable.

The simulator logs every request it has to clip, which makes the difference measurable rather than rhetorical. Across the thesis run, the XP policy asked for more transmission time than a slot contains in 4,601 agent-slots — a median request of 7,270 seconds against a 3,600-second slot, peaking at 16,081 — and over-committed data it did not have in another 2,491. Every XT run logged nothing at all. The analytical LAAO solver sits between the two, but for a different reason: its 998 warnings top out at 3,689 seconds, a 2.5% overshoot from bisection tolerance rather than a structural mistake.

Trade-off: XT is not free. Because it spreads transfers over the full slot at the lowest workable power, it moves data less aggressively than XP and carries a slightly higher average backlog — bounded and stable, but higher. What it buys is lower carbon and a policy whose chosen action is the action actually executed, instead of one the environment quietly rewrites before crediting the reward.

03 · Results

What the four methods actually traded

1,000 hours of held-out 2025 carbon data, Poisson arrivals averaging 1,250 MB per server per hour, against the published DWPA baseline.

MethodTotal carbon (g)Avg. queue (MB)vs. baseline
DWPA — published baseline4090.333323.63—

HACRO — analytical compute, learned offload3218.112649.87−21% carbon, −20% queue
RMAPPO — fully joint3362.182516.96−18% carbon, −24% queue

The honest reading is that the lowest carbon number is not the proposed method's. HACRO — analytical CPU-frequency allocation bolted onto a learned offloading policy — beat RMAPPO on emissions by about 4%, because its closed form targets the cubic cost of CPU frequency directly and deterministically. It paid for that with a queue that oscillates: splitting the decision means the compute half never accounts for what the offload half is about to do. RMAPPO, deciding both jointly, gave back a little carbon for the steadiest backlog of the four. LAAO shows the same tension from the other side — strictly better carbon than DWPA, at a slightly worse queue.

04 · The codebase

One simulator, thirty-four configurations

Comparing methods honestly means every method has to run against the same environment, the same traces, and the same constraint checks.

The repository is about 5,200 lines of Python: a discrete-time cloud-edge simulator that owns the physics — queue dynamics, Shannon-rate transmission, cubic CPU energy, carbon accounting — behind one solve(state) interface, so an analytical algorithm and a trained policy are interchangeable at the call site. Every request that would break a physical constraint gets clipped and logged rather than silently applied, which is what makes the log comparison in section 02 possible.

  • Config-driven runsA string like MARPPO_XT_CTDE selects algorithm, action decoder, and centralized-vs-decentralized training; the thesis run list is 34 such configurations, with weights cached per configuration and trained on demand when missing.
  • Workload modelsSix arrival processes — Poisson, uniform and exponential heterogeneity, Zipf ranking, on/off bursts, constant rate — plus a surge window used for the burst-traffic experiment.
  • LoggingPer-slot CSV and text logs of every queue, allocation, power, energy and emission figure, plus the constraint-warning log that produced the numbers in section 02.
On attribution: all of this code is mine, but not all of the algorithms are. DWPA is a reimplementation of the baseline this work builds on, and three further comparison methods reimplement other published approaches so they could be measured under identical conditions. LAAO, the RMAPPO variants, and the XT-Decoder are the thesis's own contributions.
